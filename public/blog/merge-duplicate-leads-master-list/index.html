How to merge duplicate leads from multiple spreadsheets into one master list

Contact lists arrived from Apollo, past campaigns and the ad platforms, and the same person could sit in three of them. Here is the master list we built for a cross-border CFO firm, and what a spreadsheet can and cannot do on its own.

Short answer

Put every list into the same columns, clean the field you match on (usually the email address), and keep one record per person in a single master list. Check each new file against that master before anything is added, and mark contacts as used the moment you export them to a channel. A spreadsheet can handle the first part; once lists arrive every week from several tools, a small custom tool like the Data Sorter we built for a cross-border CFO firm keeps the checks and the usage record in one place.

Why duplicate leads cost more than they look

The client is a cross-border finance firm. It runs cold email and paid campaigns on Meta and LinkedIn from the same pool of contacts. Those contacts came from three places: Apollo exports, past campaigns re-enriched in Clay, and data from the Meta and LinkedIn ad platforms. Each arrived as its own file.

The same person could sit in two or three of those files at once. Every copy cost something. It might be a sending slot, an export credit, or ad budget spent showing the same message to the same founder twice. And with no usage record, a channel could contact someone it had already used.

None of this is unusual. Most teams that buy or build lists end up with one spreadsheet per source and a habit of comparing them by hand. That is fine at a few hundred rows. It stops being fine when the outbound plan targets 10,000 to 15,000 emails a month to roughly 3,000 to 4,000 people, across eight audience segments that each need fresh contacts. That was the programme Data Sorter was built to support.

How to merge duplicate leads from multiple spreadsheets

If you only have a handful of files, you do not need software. A spreadsheet will do, as long as you follow the same steps every time.

  1. Use the same columns everywhere. Before merging, make every list match one layout: name, email, company, country, source, date added. Exports from different tools label the same field differently, so map them first.
  2. Clean the field you match on. Trim spaces and put emails in lower case. Under the email standard, RFC 5321, the domain part is not case-sensitive. The part before the @ technically can be, but in practice two addresses that differ only in case almost always reach the same person, so most teams treat them as one.
  3. Pick one match key and stick to it. Email is the usual choice. Name plus company is a weaker fallback, because people change jobs and spell their names in different ways.
  4. Stack the lists and remove duplicates. Paste everything into one sheet with a column for the source, then keep one row per email. In Google Sheets the UNIQUE function returns the distinct rows; Excel has a Remove Duplicates button that does the same job.
  5. Add a status column. Fresh or used, and which channel used them. Without this, the merge is clean today and messy again next week.

Step five is where spreadsheets struggle. Removing duplicates tells you nothing about history. Someone has to remember to update the status after every export, and the day they forget is the day a contact gets the same pitch twice.

What we built for the firm

We built Data Sorter, an internal tool with six screens, each doing one job. We started by mapping every source of contacts and every channel that uses them. Then we defined one master record per contact, with a usage status and a history of exports. Only after that did we build the screens.

Upload and Compare: two checks before anything is added

Every new list is checked against the master database. Two checks run on every upload: is this contact already in the master, and has this contact already been used? Both are flagged before anything is added, so the team decides what to do with them instead of finding out after a campaign has gone out.

Master Data: one list in place of a spreadsheet per source

All contacts live in one searchable list with filters. The marketing team no longer opens four files to answer one question. Every channel draws from the same list, so everyone sees the same counts and statuses.

Segments built with filters

Audiences are built with filters for country, industry and usage status. A campaign for founders in one country starts from relevant, unused contacts rather than from several files joined by hand.

Export by channel, with a usage rule

Data Sorter exports for four channels: outbound email, Meta Ads, LinkedIn Ads and newsletters. When contacts are exported to a channel, they are marked as used at that moment. The next audience for that channel can filter them out, which helps avoid pitching the same person twice.

Dashboard, history and backup

The dashboard shows total records, fresh and used contacts, channel usage and audience breakdowns, such as founders by country, on one screen. Every upload and export is logged with its date, so the team can see which list came from where and what went to each channel. There is a settings page and a backup option.

Why the usage record matters as much as the merge

Most advice about duplicate leads stops at removing them. In this project the harder problem was repeat contact across channels. A person can be unique in the master list and still be emailed in March and shown a LinkedIn ad with the same offer in April, simply because nobody wrote down the first contact.

This matters for sending too. Mailbox providers watch how people react to your mail. Google’s email sender guidelines ask senders to keep spam complaints low and to send only to people who want the mail. Emailing the same person repeatedly from different lists is an easy way to collect complaints you did not need.

Credits were the other reason. Apollo export credits are limited and may run as an annual pool, so past campaign contacts had value. Data Sorter lets the team re-check and reuse them with care, instead of buying the same people again.

What we learned building it

Decide what counts as one person before writing any code. The record design came before the screens. If the match rule is vague, no amount of software fixes it.

Record usage at export, not later by hand. The moment a file leaves for a channel is the only moment you can be sure what was sent. Anything that relies on someone updating a column afterwards will drift.

Keep history, even when it feels unnecessary. The upload and export log is the least exciting screen. It is also the one that answers “where did this contact come from?” when something goes wrong.

Not every team needs this. If you add one or two lists a month and use a single channel, a well-kept spreadsheet with a status column is enough. We would tell you that on the first call. A tool like Data Sorter earns its place when lists arrive from several tools, several channels draw from them, and credits or ad budget are limited.

Frequently asked questions

How do I merge duplicate leads from multiple spreadsheets?

Put every list into the same columns, add a column for the source, clean the email field (trim spaces, lower case), stack the lists in one sheet and keep one row per email. In Google Sheets the UNIQUE function does this; Excel has a Remove Duplicates button. Add a status column so you know who has already been contacted.

What is the best field to match duplicate contacts on?

Email address is the most reliable key, once it is trimmed and put in lower case. Name plus company can work as a fallback, but it misses people who changed jobs or spell their names differently.

Can Excel or Google Sheets remove duplicate leads automatically?

They can remove duplicates inside one sheet when you run the command or formula. They do not check new files against your history on their own, and they do not record which channel has already used a contact. That part needs a process or a small tool.

When do I need a lead deduplication tool instead of a spreadsheet?

When lists arrive regularly from several tools, more than one channel draws from them, and repeat contact or wasted credits start to cost you. If you add one or two lists a month for one channel, a spreadsheet with a status column is usually enough.
